MESA MBA C4 | TERM 2 | CASE 3 | SEED DATA All In Capital - Past Deal Memos

This pack contains twelve deal memos from All In Capital's past evaluations: six companies the fund backed and six it passed on. Treat them as the fund's own record of how it has reasoned. Read them as the fund's decisions, written by the people who made them.

Memos describe each company, the founders as the partners experienced them, the risks, and the reason for the final call. The founder assessment is subjective by design. The fund treats the person as a primary variable and writes it down.

Memo 1: The ePlane Company

Sector: Deeptech (aerial mobility) | Stage: Early round | Source: Warm intro, Fund I founder network | Decision: INVESTED

What it does. Electric vertical take-off and landing (eVTOL) aircraft and unmanned aerial vehicles for short-range intracity travel. Incubated at IIT Madras, grown out of a combustion research lab; founded 2016.

Founder assessment. Pranjal Mehta (CEO) and Satyanarayanan Chakravarthy (CTO). The pairing is the strongest part of the file: deep technical credibility on one side, a commercial operator on the other, and both have stayed with the problem for years rather than chasing a trend. Mehta is the better storyteller; Chakravarthy is quieter but the one people in the room defer to on technical questions. Concern: neither has shipped a certified aircraft, and neither has run a large manufacturing organisation.

Risks and open questions. Regulation and certification timelines are long and outside the founders' control. Capital intensity is high and the first revenue is far away. Market sizing in the deck was aspirational rather than evidenced.

Why we invested. We were not underwriting the market size in the deck. We were underwriting two people who had stayed with a hard problem for years and who answered 'what would make you quit?' with a specific technical milestone rather than a platitude. The thesis fit is deeptech at the earliest stage, where the founder is most of the investment. We accepted that this is a long-duration bet and sized the cheque so a slow regulatory path does not force a bad financing.

Memo 2: Petalry

Sector: Consumer brand (D2C beauty) | Stage: Pre-seed | Source: Public application form | Decision: PASSED

What it does. Direct-to-consumer skincare brand selling through its own site and marketplaces. Roughly 18 months old, a few thousand monthly orders, strong Instagram presence.

Founder assessment. Anika Rao, solo founder, formerly a brand manager at a large FMCG company. Articulate, polished, and clearly good at brand and creative. She was candid that she had never owned a P\&L and had no co-founder covering operations or supply chain. In the call she answered unit-economics questions with channel-level averages, not cohort data. Our read: a strong marketer, not yet a business builder, and unaware of the gap.

Risks and open questions. Customer acquisition cost rising month on month; roughly two thirds of orders from paid social. Repeat rate not tracked. Product made by a single contract manufacturer. Category is crowded with better-funded incumbents.

Why we passed. A real brand with real orders, which is why it reached a call. We passed for two linked reasons. The numbers showed growth being bought rather than earned, and the founder could not tell us the repeat rate. The second reason matters more: consumer-brand investing at our stage turns on whether the founder instinctively thinks in cohorts and margins, and in this call she did not. If she returns with six months of cohort data and an operations co-founder, we would look again.

Memo 3: Unbox Robotics

Sector: Deeptech (warehouse robotics) | Stage: Early round | Source: Warm intro, co-investor | Decision: INVESTED

What it does. Compact, AI-assisted robotic package-sorting systems for the warehouses and fulfilment centres of e-commerce and logistics companies. Pune-based, founded 2019.

Founder assessment. Pramod Ghadge (CEO) and Shahid Memon (CTO). A clean split: one builds the company, the other builds the machine. What impressed us in the meeting was how little they relied on the deck; both talked about specific customer sites and what broke during pilots. They described failures before we asked. Concern: the CEO was still learning to sell to large enterprise procurement teams, and hardware hiring in Pune was not yet proven.

Risks and open questions. Long enterprise sales cycles, pilot-to-rollout conversion uncertain, hardware margin structure unproven at scale, and several better-funded global competitors.

Why we invested. This is deeptech with a nearer path to revenue than most. A concrete customer pain (sorting cost and accuracy), a product already in live sites, and founders who treated pilots as learning, not marketing. We were comfortable that the pilot-conversion risk was the right risk to be carrying at this stage.

Memo 4: HopBox

Sector: Consumer tech (10-minute delivery) | Stage: Pre-seed | Source: Warm intro | Decision: PASSED

What it does. Hyperlocal delivery of groceries and daily essentials in under ten minutes from small dark stores, operating in one city neighbourhood cluster.

Founder assessment. Varun Malhotra and Dev Sethi, two former operations managers at a quick-commerce company. Their operational knowledge was real and detailed; they knew picking times, rider utilisation and wastage by category. They were also visibly energetic. But when pressed, neither could describe a path to profitability that did not depend on a large raise to reach density, and they treated that as someone else's problem to solve.

Risks and open questions. Capital-intensive model that only works at density. Well-funded incumbents with deep pockets. Contribution margin negative on the data shown. Customer loyalty unproven.

Why we passed. Good operators, wrong game for a pre-seed fund. The model only works with far more capital than we deploy, and we did not want to be the small cheque that gets diluted or squeezed once larger funds set the terms. The founders were strong; this was a pass on structure, not on people. We told them so and said we would reconsider if they found a dense, narrower niche with positive contribution margin.

Memo 5: Wint Wealth

Sector: Fintech (bonds / fixed income) | Stage: Early round | Source: Founder referral | Decision: INVESTED

What it does. A platform that makes bond-market products accessible to retail investors in India. Early on it was run almost manually.

Founder assessment. Ajinkya Kulkarni, founder and CEO. His early approach is what we backed: he and his co-founders talked to prospective users directly, explained bonds to them, ran a basic lead-generation website and arranged transactions by hand, even joining intro calls themselves. We read that as the right instinct. They did not build a product around an assumption; they sat across the table from the customer first. Concern: the early manual model had no obvious scale mechanism, and the regulatory perimeter for retail bond distribution was evolving.

Risks and open questions. Regulation of retail access to debt products. Trust is slow to build in a category where mistakes cost real money. Early volumes were small.

Why we invested. Fintech with a real pain point (retail investors cannot easily buy quality bonds) and a founder doing unscalable things in the right order. Founder-market fit was the deciding factor, along with how clearly he understood that trust, not technology, was the bottleneck.

Memo 6: CampusCredit

Sector: Fintech (student credit) | Stage: Pre-seed | Source: Public application form | Decision: PASSED

What it does. Buy-now-pay-later and small-ticket credit for college students, underwritten with parent co-signer data and campus ambassador networks.

Founder assessment. Rohan Iyer, 24, recent engineering graduate, and a classmate as CTO. Smart, hungry, strong on distribution through campus networks. But neither had any experience in credit, collections or risk, and Iyer dismissed our questions on default rates as 'something the NBFC partner handles'. That answer decided it: a lending founder who does not own risk is a distribution business with a lender's liabilities.

Risks and open questions. Depends on an NBFC partner for the licence and balance sheet. Collections from students are hard. Unclear credit-bureau data on first-time borrowers. Regulatory scrutiny of digital lending.

Why we passed. Fintech is core to our thesis, but this one fails on the founder question. The product itself was reasonable. A team that does not own risk will find out the cost of that the expensive way, and with someone else's money. We encouraged them to hire a credit lead before raising and offered an introduction to a risk operator.

Memo 7: Giva

Sector: Consumer brand (jewellery, D2C) | Stage: Early round | Source: Public application form | Decision: INVESTED

What it does. An online-first fine silver jewellery brand founded in 2019, later expanding into gold and lab-grown diamonds and building offline presence.

Founder assessment. Ishendra Agarwal, co-founder, an IIT Kanpur alumnus. In contrast to the typical D2C founder we meet, the story he told was about repeat purchase, gifting occasions and product margin, not follower counts. He knew his cost of acquisition by channel, his return rate and the percentage of revenue from repeat customers. That fluency is rare at this stage. Concern: the category had a risk of becoming a discount-led marketplace game, and inventory-heavy models punish any forecasting error.

Risks and open questions. Inventory and working-capital intensity. Heavy reliance on marketplaces and paid channels early. Competition from established jewellers going digital.

Why we invested. Our D2C passes usually fail on repeat rate. This one passed the test we apply to every brand: the founder could tell us cohort repeat behaviour and gross margin without looking at notes. A consumer-brand cheque at pre-seed is only defensible when the founder thinks like an operator, and this one did.

Memo 8: AeroDrop

Sector: Deeptech (drone delivery) | Stage: Pre-seed | Source: Warm intro | Decision: PASSED

What it does. Autonomous drone delivery of medical supplies and small parcels over short distances, with a working prototype and a handful of demonstration flights.

Founder assessment. Dr. Meera Kapoor, an aerospace PhD with a strong research record, and an operations co-founder who joined recently. Technically excellent and deeply convincing on the flight systems. She had less to say about who the paying customer was, who bore liability for a failed delivery, and how regulators would permit routine beyond-visual-line-of-sight operation. She viewed those as 'solved once the tech works'.

Risks and open questions. Regulatory permission for commercial operations was uncertain on a multi-year horizon. No signed customer. Liability and insurance unaddressed. Weak commercial co-founder.

Why we passed. We like deeptech, and she is a high-quality technologist. We passed because the file had no customer, no path through the regulator and no answer on liability, and the founder treated all three as secondary. In the ePlane case the regulatory path was equally long, but the founders had a clear view of it and a concrete milestone sequence. Here there was none. We would reconsider with a signed pilot customer and a regulator-facing plan.

Memo 9: SuperK

Sector: Consumer tech (small-town retail) | Stage: Early round | Source: Warm intro, Fund I founder | Decision: INVESTED

What it does. A tech-enabled grocery retail network for small towns. It rebrands existing neighbourhood stores under its own name and provides standardised pricing, supply, discounts and digital billing. Founded 2020, operating in small towns of Andhra Pradesh.

Founder assessment. Anil Thontepu and Neeraj Menta. Thontepu came from growth and product work at a Southeast Asian services marketplace; Menta had co-founded a cafeteria-management company and worked in fintech. A complementary pair: one thinks in customer acquisition and product, the other in operations and building a business from nothing. Both were willing to spend months in small-town stores learning how shopkeepers actually operate, which is not the usual profile of a metro-based tech founder. Concern: rural supply-chain execution is tough and neither had run a retail network at scale.

Risks and open questions. Operationally intense, thin margins, store-owner trust and retention, supply chain in small towns. Limited data on same-store sales growth at the time.

Why we invested. Founder behaviour decided it. They had visited dozens of stores and could describe what the owners wanted: working capital, price support, a branded look, not 'technology'. A franchise-style model with real field execution is hard to copy. We saw the risk, but it was the right sort of risk for a founder pair that was already on the ground.

Memo 10: Coinlane

Sector: Web3 (crypto exchange and wallet) | Stage: Pre-seed | Source: Public application form | Decision: PASSED

What it does. A retail crypto trading app and wallet for Indian users, with fiat on-ramp through a payment partner. Growing user base from referral campaigns.

Founder assessment. Siddharth Menon, previously a growth lead at a payments company, and a co-founder with a blockchain engineering background. Capable and fast-moving; the user growth was real. In the meeting he spoke confidently about regulation, but his answers amounted to a bet that the rules would become friendlier. There was no plan for the scenario in which they did not.

Risks and open questions. Regulatory and tax uncertainty on crypto in India. Banking and payment-partner dependency. Trading revenue is cyclical. Intense competition from large, well-funded exchanges.

Why we passed. This is outside the areas we back, and no founder quality could change that. Our decision was about the category, not the people: a business whose existence depends on a favourable regulatory change is not one we want to underwrite at pre-seed. We declined quickly and said why.

Memo 11: Jai Kisan

Sector: Fintech (rural / agri credit) | Stage: Early round | Source: Warm intro | Decision: INVESTED

What it does. A fintech platform providing financing to farmers and rural households for income-generating purchases such as agricultural and dairy equipment. Mumbai-based, founded 2017.

Founder assessment. Arjun Ahluwalia and Adriel Maniego. The case for them was in how they built distribution: a presence across many channel-partner locations in multiple districts, with thousands of farmers registered and assessed before they scaled lending. That says they started with the borrower, not the app. Concern raised internally: credit-risk depth would need to be built or hired quickly as lending scaled.

Risks and open questions. Rural credit risk and collections, regulatory exposure, dependence on channel partners, and seasonality tied to monsoon and crop cycles.

Why we invested. This is the harder cousin of the student-credit pass. Like CampusCredit, it is a lending business, but these founders had done the unglamorous groundwork in the field and the discussion centred on how they would build credit-risk depth. Our deciding question was whether the founders treated risk as theirs. Here they did. In the discussion, one partner asked whether the thesis stretched to rural credit; the group agreed the founder behaviour mattered more than the sector label.

Memo 12: Prepnest

Sector: Consumer tech (exam-prep) | Stage: Pre-seed | Source: Warm intro | Decision: PASSED

What it does. A mobile-first test-preparation app for competitive exams, with video lessons and adaptive practice. Strong early download numbers.

Founder assessment. Neha Bhatt, a former teacher with a popular online following, and a technical co-founder. Her teaching ability is excellent and the audience is real. She is clearly passionate. However, on retention, her answer was that students 'keep coming back because the content is great', and she did not have 30- or 90-day numbers. She also bristled at a question about what happens once a larger player copies the format.

Risks and open questions. Crowded category with entrenched, heavily funded players. Retention and conversion to paid not shown. Content-led, founder-dependent product that is hard to scale beyond one teacher.

Why we passed. The founder is likeable and talented, and the audience was real. We passed because the product is the teacher, and the data did not show it travelling beyond her. Defensibility was weak and she did not seem to want to examine that. A founder who gets defensive on the questions that matter is a signal in itself.
